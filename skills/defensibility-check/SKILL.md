# The Defensibility Check

## The move

For every claim-making sentence in a piece of content, ask one question:

**"What specifically do you mean?"**

If the answer to that question requires a new abstraction — another word like "throughout," "across," "holistically," "compounding" — the sentence has failed. The language is doing the work that evidence should be doing.

A defensible sentence survives the question by pointing at something concrete: a person, an action, a specific thing, a measurable change.

## Why this matters for Nick

Experienced reviewers spot undefendable marketing language instantly because it can't survive a "what do you mean?" question. "Scale up many different parts throughout the process" fails — it's a verb being used as a value prop with no defensible specifics underneath.

This is the skill that separates marketing that practitioners trust from marketing they dismiss. Nick's ICP (bioprocessing scientists, process engineers, R&D digital leaders) has been burned by vendor language for years. They read undefendable claims as either naïve or dishonest.

Building this muscle in Nick's reviewing practice means every piece of content — from any source — gets filtered through the same test before it goes out.

## How to apply it

When Nick shares content for review, do not simply critique it. Walk through it *with him*, applying the move out loud so he can internalize the pattern.

For each claim-making sentence:

1. Read it back to him.
2. Ask: "What does this specifically mean?"
3. If the answer requires abstraction-swapping, flag the failure and propose a concrete rewrite.
4. If the answer points at something specific, confirm it passes.

Do this even when the sentence seems fine at first glance. The goal is repetition — Nick should start anticipating the question before you ask it.

## Failure patterns to watch for

- **Verb-as-noun claims**: "scale up," "optimize," "accelerate" used as standalone value props with no object.
- **Abstract compound nouns**: "compounding insights," "seamless integration," "end-to-end visibility." These hide who, what, when.
- **Actor-less verbs**: "innovation happens," "value is unlocked," "insights emerge." Rewrite to name the person doing the thing.
- **Range-words doing specificity work**: "throughout," "across," "holistic," "comprehensive." These substitute scope for substance.
- **Upgrade vocabulary**: language that sounds more sophisticated than the underlying idea warrants. The McKinsey-deck test — if it would appear in one without modification, rewrite it.

## What to resist

- Do not rewrite silently. Nick needs to see the failing sentence and the rewrite side by side, so he learns the pattern.
- Do not score content holistically ("this is 7/10"). Go sentence by sentence on the claim-making ones.
- Do not apply the test to every sentence. Transitions, connective tissue, and setup don't make claims. The test applies specifically to sentences that assert value or argue a point.
- Do not let Nick off the hook when his own writing fails the test. The skill is worthless if it only catches other people's failures.

## The self-diagnostic

The real goal is for Nick to stop needing this skill.

When Nick has internalized the move, he will start asking "what specifically do I mean?" *while writing*, which will change his drafts before they ever get reviewed. That's the endpoint — the skill makes itself progressively less necessary as the reviewing instinct becomes the writing instinct.

Track this. If Nick's drafts over time contain fewer flaggable sentences, the skill is working. If reviewer pushback on language decreases, even better. That's the real outcome measure.
