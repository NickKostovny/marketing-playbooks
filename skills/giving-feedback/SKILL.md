# Giving Feedback

Grounded in Adam Grant's framework: criticism buried in compliments backfires. The feedback sandwich — praise, criticism, praise — fails because opening praise seems insincere (people brace for the blow) and closing praise buries the message via primacy/recency effects. The recipient remembers first and last; the criticism gets lost in the middle.

## Core principle

Be radically candid. Make the feedback feel like an act of investment, not judgment.

## Four-step framework

### 1. Explain why you're giving the feedback
Signal that you're in their corner, not above them. This single move makes feedback ~40% more effective:

> "I'm sharing this because I think highly of your work and I know you can reach that level."

Frame it around their potential and your belief in them — not your authority or expertise.

### 2. Take yourself off a pedestal
Reduce the power differential. Make it a peer conversation, not a verdict from on high:

> "I've benefited a lot from people pushing me, and I want to pay that forward."
> "I'm still working on this myself — I thought if I noticed it, it might be useful to hear."

This is especially important when Nick (as a first-time Head of Marketing) is giving feedback up, across, or to senior practitioners who may be skeptical.

### 3. Ask if the person wants feedback
Giving people agency dramatically reduces defensiveness:

> "I noticed a couple of things and wondered if you'd want some feedback."

No one has ever said no to this phrasing. Ownership over the choice to receive = openness to the content.

### 4. Have a transparent dialogue, not a manipulative monologue
Don't stage-manage the conversation. State what you observed, name your concern, check if they saw it the same way, then co-decide what to do:

> "Here's what I observed. Here's why it concerned me. Do you see it the same way? Here's what I think we might do — what do you think?"

The goal is joint diagnosis, not delivery of a verdict.

## Applying to Nick's context

When Nick is preparing feedback for bioprocessing practitioners or technical collaborators, two additional considerations apply:

- **Credibility gap is real**: Practitioners may be skeptical of marketing perspective on their domain. Lean heavily into steps 2 and 3 — humility and consent disarm this.
- **Be specific and observational**: "The way this was described in the job posting" not "your messaging is unclear." Practitioners respond to concrete evidence, not abstract critique.

## Quick diagnostic

Before helping Nick draft feedback, ask or infer:
- Who is the recipient and what's the power dynamic?
- What's the specific behavior or output being addressed?
- What outcome does Nick want from the conversation?

Then apply the four steps to shape the approach.
