# Writing Craft

Concision, active voice, specifics over abstractions, and cutting throat-clearing are default behavior —
they are not listed here. This file carries only the arbitrary choices, which I'd otherwise guess at.

## House conventions

- **Oxford comma: always.** "We invited the strippers, JFK, and Stalin."
- **US English, no trailing s:** toward, backward, afterward, forward.
- **Exclamation points: almost never.** One per document maximum, reserved for genuine exclamations.
  If emphasis needs a mark, reorder the sentence instead.
- **Emphatic word last.** End position carries the weight: "…but it has hardly advanced in fortitude."

## Grammar calls worth getting right

- **Comma before *because* flips the meaning.** "Rhonda didn't do it because she loved him" (she did it,
  not for love) vs. "Rhonda didn't do it, because she loved him" (she didn't; love is the reason).
  Usually you want the comma.
- **"And so" — drop the *and*.** Both are conjunctions; keep *so*.
- **Comma + conjunction only between two independent clauses.** "He ate all the food, and went back for
  more" is wrong — the second clause has no subject. Drop the comma.
- **Dependent clause first takes a comma; second doesn't.** "As the wave crashed down, the surfer fell."
  / "The surfer fell as the wave crashed down." (*since, while, because, although, as*)

## Two editing rules that beat instinct

- **Zweig's two-thirds.** Take the draft's word count, multiply by ⅔ — that's the ceiling. Short-form
  (Slack, email): cut by half on the first pass.
- **Kill your favorite line.** Whatever you like most in your own writing is usually what readers like
  least. The clever phrase is the first cut.

## Marketing copy: the shape Nick actually ships

Encoded from his hand-rewrite of a LinkedIn post (2026-07). Each item is my draft → his cut. These are
observed preferences from one substantial rewrite, not laws — if a later rewrite contradicts one, update it.

- **No antithesis openers.** I wrote "The hard part isn't the judgment call. It's assembling the data to
  make it." He wrote "One of the hardest parts of a deviation investigation is assembling the data to make
  it." Setup-across-two-sentences reads as a writerly tic. Say it once, in one sentence.
- **Hedge the problem, sharpen the benefit.** My instinct runs the other way. He softened every claim a
  practitioner could counterexample ("the hard part" → "one of the hardest parts"; "the evidence lives in"
  → "the evidence you need is often spread across") and then made a benefit claim larger than any I'd have
  passed: **"Invert gives everyone the power of an SME."** Absolute claims about the customer's world are
  exposure. Absolute claims about what Invert does for them are the product.
- **Mine the friction detail for the benefit line.** I used "larger queries need an SME with different
  system access" only as pain. He flipped that same detail into the value proposition. When a problem
  sentence names a scarce person, role, or access level, the benefit line is usually "everyone gets that."
- **Second person.** "The evidence you need," not "the evidence needed."
- **Copy is a caption for a visual, not a substitute for it.** He replaced my prose description of the
  capability with "Here's an example of…" — the post exists to hand off to a demo clip. Default assumption
  for marketing copy: something visual is coming. Write the handoff, not the replacement.
- **Cut the problem chain if it delays the product.** He deleted the most heavily sourced material in the
  draft — the 48-hour window, open-high-and-park, three records become six, retest timing, patient impact.
  All verbatim-backed. All gone. This sits in real tension with content-proportion-check's "practitioner is
  protagonist, Invert in the methods section." His revealed preference is shorter and faster to Invert.
  Lead with the short version; offer the problem-led long version as the alternate, don't default to it.
- **Close on business consequence, flatly.** Mine: "The investigation is still theirs. The search for the
  data doesn't have to be." His: "It matters how long deviation investigations take. Each hour spent costs
  real money." Blunter, and worse as prose — that's the point. No writerly turn in the final line.
- **"Kill your favorite line" already covered this, and I ignored it twice.** Both closers I wrote were
  favorite lines; both were cut. The rule bites hardest on the last sentence. Apply it there first.

One thing to flag rather than silently inherit: he closed on cost, but the sourced practitioner led with
patient delay, not money. For a cost-negative site building an NPV case, money is plausibly the better
buying trigger — but it was a substitution, so don't assume it generalizes to every audience.
